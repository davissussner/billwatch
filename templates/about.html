How this works

This site makes two claims that deserve scrutiny: that these are the bills that mattered, and that the summaries are fair. Here is exactly how both are produced, so you can decide whether you believe them.

How bills are chosen

Congress introduced {{ stats.bills }}-plus bills in the period covered here. Almost none become law. Rather than guess at importance, each bill is scored on what actually happened to it in a given month.

A bill's score is driven by the single most consequential action it saw that month — not the number of actions. A bill referred to six committees has not done more than a bill that passed the House.

Became law100
Sent to the President60
Passed a chamber40
Reported out of committee20
Markup or hearing10
Amendment activity5
Introduced or referred3

Then adjustments: up to 10 points for cosponsors (log-scaled, so the 400th cosponsor counts less than the 4th), 8 points if at least a fifth of cosponsors come from the other party, and 5 points if a companion bill moved the same month.

Finally, ceremonial bills are multiplied by 0.15. Resolutions designating a National Something Week, and bills naming a post office or a VA clinic, pass easily and often become law. Without the penalty they crowd out everything else. This is the most debatable judgment on this page, and it is applied by pattern-matching bill titles, which means it will occasionally be wrong in both directions.

Every bill page shows its own score arithmetic.

How summaries are written

Summaries are generated by claude-sonnet-5 reading the actual bill text — not the news coverage, not the press releases. Where the Congressional Research Service has written a summary, that non-partisan text is supplied alongside the bill as grounding.

The model is required to return a fixed structure rather than free prose. This is deliberate: open-ended prose drifts toward advocacy, and fixed fields do not. The "points of contention" field requires an attribution for every claim, which makes an unattributed opinion impossible to emit. The site never says which side is right.

Each bill page records which model wrote the summary, which prompt version was used, and which version of the bill text was read — a bill as introduced can differ sharply from the same bill as passed.

Limits you should know about

  • These summaries are machine-generated. They are not reviewed by a person before publication. Check the bill text before relying on one for anything that matters.
  • Momentum is not importance. A bill that dies in committee can matter more than one that passes unanimously. This site measures movement, which is a proxy, and an imperfect one.
  • Very long bills — appropriations, the NDAA — are summarized in sections and composed. Detail is lost in that process.
  • The ceremonial filter is a heuristic on bill titles and will misfire sometimes.
  • Bills can be selected before they are summarized. Those pages say so rather than hiding the gap. Currently {{ stats.summarized }} of {{ stats.selections }} are summarized.

Data

Bill records, action histories, sponsors, and CRS summaries come from GovInfo BILLSTATUS bulk data, refreshed daily. Bill text is fetched from GovInfo. The Congress.gov API is used for incremental updates.

Bulk data is the primary source because the Congress.gov API filters on record update time rather than action date, so it cannot cleanly answer "what moved in July 2026." The bulk action histories can.

Running it locally

Search needs to fetch a JSON index, which browsers block for pages opened directly from disk with a file:// URL. Serve the folder over HTTP instead:

cd site && python3 -m http.server 8000

Then open http://localhost:8000. Everything except search works fine from disk.
